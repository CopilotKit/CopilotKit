### 1. The four strokes

The piston slides up and down inside the cylinder. Two valves at the top let gas in (intake, blue) and out (exhaust, red), and a spark plug sits between them:

Then the cycle starts again. In real engines the valves open slightly before and close slightly after these exact points, so the gas keeps flowing smoothly at speed.

### 2. How up-and-down becomes rotation

The piston is linked to the **crankshaft** by a connecting rod, a bit like your legs turning bicycle pedals. Every push of the piston becomes a turn of the crankshaft, which drives the gearbox and finally the wheels.

Only the power stroke actually pushes. A heavy **flywheel**, and in a multi-cylinder engine the other cylinders firing in turn, carries the crankshaft through the other three strokes. A four-cylinder engine is set up so that one cylinder is always on its power stroke.

### 3. Timing

One full cycle takes **two turns of the crankshaft** (720°), so each cylinder fires once every second turn. A **camshaft**, geared to spin at half the crankshaft's speed, opens each valve at exactly the right moment, and the engine computer times the fuel injection and the spark. The numbers get big fast:

### 4. Why squeeze it first?

Compressing the mixture before lighting it makes the burn much more forceful and gets far more work out of the same fuel. Petrol engines typically compress to around **10:1 to 12:1**, about a tenth of the original volume. Diesel engines go further, roughly **15:1 to 22:1**, so the air gets hot enough to ignite the fuel on its own, which is why a diesel has no spark plugs.

It's controlled burning, not an explosion. Even so, most of the fuel's energy ends up as heat rather than motion, which is why the engine needs a radiator and coolant. More detail: [Four-stroke engine on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-stroke_engine).
